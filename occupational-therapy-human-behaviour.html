HEALTHCARE & HUMAN BEHAVIOUR

What Occupational Therapy Taught Me About Human Behaviour

Occupational therapy has taught me that people’s behaviour rarely tells the whole story.

When someone finds an everyday task difficult, it is tempting to focus on what they can or cannot do. But understanding the person means looking at more than the task itself. Their experiences, circumstances, environment and what matters to them can all shape how they manage.

That has changed the way I think about human behaviour. It has also given me a perspective I now bring to user experience.

There is usually more going on than you can see

People do not always find it easy to explain what is getting in their way. They might be worried, tired, in pain, unsure what is expected, or dealing with several things at once.

From the outside, a person's response might seem confusing. They may appear reluctant, frustrated or uninterested. But there could be a reason that is not obvious at first.

Occupational therapy taught me to stay curious. What is happening for this person? What matters to them? What might be making this feel difficult?

It is a reminder not to jump straight to conclusions about someone based on one moment or one decision.

The same task can feel different to different people

A task that seems simple to one person can feel much harder to another. People have different abilities, experiences, routines and priorities. What feels manageable in one situation might not feel manageable in another.

The task matters, but so does the person doing it and the circumstances around them. A small change in support, surroundings or approach can affect how someone experiences it.

That has taught me to look at the whole situation, rather than assume there is one answer that works for everyone.

Listening helps you understand what matters

It is easy to assume we know what someone needs. But what seems important from the outside may not be what matters most to them.

Taking the time to listen can reveal what they are trying to achieve, what they are concerned about and what a good outcome would look like from their point of view.

That understanding makes it easier to find an approach that feels relevant to the person, rather than simply offering the solution we expected to give.

How this connects to user experience

I see a similar principle in UX: people experience something from their own point of view, with their own needs and questions.

A website might seem clear to the person who built it because they already know the business and understand how everything fits together. A new visitor does not have that background. They may be trying to work out what the business does, whether it is right for them and what to do next.

If the information is unclear or difficult to find, it can create an unnecessary barrier. The business may have the right answer, but the visitor still has to work too hard to reach it.

Understanding people changes how you communicate

My OT background has made me think carefully about the gap between what someone intends to communicate and what another person actually understands.

That matters in writing and UX. Clear communication starts with the person receiving it: what they already know, what they might be unsure about and what they need to understand next.

It means being willing to look at the experience from their side, not only from the business's point of view.

That is the connection I keep coming back to. Whether I'm thinking about an everyday activity or a digital experience, the starting point is the same: understand what the person is trying to do, then consider what might make it easier.
